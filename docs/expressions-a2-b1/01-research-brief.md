# How many words does a Thai learner need to "cover" B1?
### Research brief for an A2→B1 American-English expressions textbook

*Compiled 2026-08-28. Every number below is either measured directly from the source list or cited to a published corpus study.*

---

## 1. The short answer

| Question | Answer | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Single words to reach B1 | **2,500–3,000 word families** | High — three independent methods converge |
| New single words in the A2→B1 jump | **~800–1,000** | High — measured |
| Phrasal verbs needed | **150** (100 productive + 50 receptive) | High — 150 covers 75%+ of all PV use |
| Collocations to teach explicitly | **300–400** | Medium — a pedagogical target, not a corpus constant |
| Fixed expressions / functional chunks | **150** | Medium — same |
| **Multi-word total for the textbook** | **≈ 600 items** | This is the book's scope |

**The headline for your book:** a student does not need "more words" to move from A2 to B1. They need **~800 new single words plus ~600 multi-word items**. The multi-word half is what actually makes them sound B1, and it is the half Thai textbooks usually skip.

---

## 2. Where the 2,500–3,000 number comes from

Three separate methods, three separate research traditions, same answer.

### Method A — Psycholinguistic testing (Milton & Alexiou)
Milton and Alexiou tested learners across CEFR levels using vocabulary-size tests. B1 learners command roughly **2,500–3,000 word families**, with **~2,750** as the typical figure. The result holds across English, French, Greek and Spanish, which suggests it is a property of the level, not of the language.

Note the implication for your students: a solid A2 learner sits around **1,000–1,500 word families**. So the A2→B1 gap is **~1,500 word families of receptive knowledge** — but only a fraction of those need explicit teaching, because many are transparent derivations (*happy → happily → unhappy*) inside a family.

### Method B — Publisher word lists (Oxford 3000, American English edition)
I downloaded the official **American English** Oxford 3000 CEFR-tagged list and counted the entries directly:

| Level | Headwords | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | 902 | 902 |
| A2 | 869 | 1,771 |
| **B1** | **809** | **2,580** |
| B2 | 730 | 3,310 |

**→ 2,580 headwords gets you through B1. The A2→B1 step is exactly 809 new headwords.**

This is the most useful number for a syllabus because it is a *countable list you can actually work through*, and because Oxford publishes an American-English version — so the spelling and word choice already match your target variety.

### Method C — Exam syllabus (Cambridge B1 Preliminary vocabulary list)
I downloaded the August 2025 official list and counted:

- **~3,366 tagged entries** (this counts each part of speech separately, so it inflates against a headword count — the real headword figure is close to Oxford's 2,580)
- **94 phrasal verbs** — that is the entire phrasal-verb expectation of the official B1 exam
- **45 entries explicitly tagged British vs American** (see §5)

**→ Cambridge's own B1 syllabus asks for fewer than 100 phrasal verbs.** If your book teaches 150, you have comfortably over-covered the level.

### Convergence
| Source | B1 figure |
|---|---|
| Milton & Alexiou (testing) | 2,500–3,000 families |
| Oxford 3000 American (cumulative to B1) | 2,580 headwords |
| Cambridge B1 Preliminary (headword equivalent) | ~2,500–3,000 |

Use **2,750 word families / ~2,600 headwords** as your book's stated target. It is defensible from all three.

## 3. Phrasal verbs: why 150 is the magic number

This is the strongest piece of evidence in the whole brief, and it should shape the book.

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| **100 phrasal verbs = 51.4% of all phrasal verb occurrences** | Gardner & Davies (2007), *TESOL Quarterly*, 100-million-word BNC |
| **Only 25 phrasal verbs = nearly one-third of all occurrences** | Gardner & Davies (2007) |
| **20 lexical verbs + 8 particles (`out, up, on, back, down, in, over, off`) = 50.4% of all phrasal verbs** | Gardner & Davies (2007) |
| **150 phrasal verbs, with their main senses, cover 75%+ of occurrences in COCA (American English)** | Garnier & Schmitt (2015), the **PHaVE List** |
| Phrasal verbs average **5.6 meaning senses**, but for most of them **2 senses cover 75%+ of uses** | Garnier & Schmitt (2015) |

### Three teaching consequences

**(a) Cap the list at 150.** Beyond 150 the return per item collapses. Teaching 400 phrasal verbs is not four times better than teaching 100 — it is barely better at all.

**(b) Teach 2 senses per phrasal verb, not 5.** This is the single most actionable finding. `pick up` has many meanings; two of them account for most real use. Thai textbooks routinely list six senses and drown the student. **Teach two. Mark the rest "recognize only."**

**(c) Organize the first unit by particle, not alphabetically.** Eight particles carry half of all phrasal verb use. A unit built around *out / up / on / back / down / in / over / off* teaches the underlying spatial metaphor once and pays off across dozens of items — far better than 26 alphabetical chapters.

**American English note:** the PHaVE List is built on **COCA — the Corpus of Contemporary American English**. This is exactly the corpus you want. Gardner & Davies is BNC (British). Where the two disagree, follow PHaVE.

## 4. Collocations: why the number is a decision, not a discovery

There is no "correct" number of collocations, because collocations are effectively unbounded.

**Shin & Nation (2008)** searched the 10-million-word spoken section of the BNC and found that **the first 100 pivot words alone yield 2,052 collocations** meeting strict frequency criteria (30+ occurrences, same sense). Their conclusion: if you stopped distinguishing single words from collocations, a very large number of collocations would qualify for inclusion in the most frequent 2,000 words of English.

So you cannot teach "all the B1 collocations." You must choose a principle. **The best principle for an A2–B1 book is the delexical verb spine.**

Twelve verbs — `make, do, take, have, get, give, go, come, put, keep, run, turn` — carry an enormous share of everyday collocation, and they are precisely the verbs Thai learners get wrong, because Thai maps several of them onto the single verb **ทำ**. Twelve verbs × ~25 partners each = **300 collocations**, and every one of them is a Thai-interference trap. Add ~100 adjective+noun, adverb+adjective and preposition patterns and you have **~400**, which is a realistic and complete-feeling book.

## 5. American English: what actually differs at this level

The good news: at A2–B1, grammar differences are negligible and the vocabulary differences are a **short, closed, teachable list**. Cambridge's own B1 list tags only **45 items** as British/American variants.

The differences that matter fall in three groups:

**(a) Nouns — everyday objects.** The classic set: *fall / autumn, movie / film, apartment / flat, elevator / lift, gas / petrol, parking lot / car park, subway / underground, trash can / dustbin, cookie / biscuit, French fries / chips, drugstore / chemist, eraser / rubber, pants / trousers, bookstore / bookshop, movie theater / cinema, jail / prison, trunk / boot, math / maths, driver's license / driving licence.*

**(b) Phrasal verbs — the ones Thai books teach British by default.** This is where your book can differentiate:

| Teach (American) | Avoid teaching as the default (British) |
|---|---|
| fill **out** a form | fill in a form |
| get **along** with someone | get on with someone |
| call someone **up** / call **back** | ring up / ring back |
| **do** the dishes | wash up |
| **clean up** your room | tidy up your room |
| **line up** | queue up |
| **take out** the trash | put out the rubbish |
| give someone a **ride** | give someone a lift |
| **turn in** your homework | hand in your homework |

**(c) Spelling and one grammar point.** *-or* not *-our* (`color, favorite, neighbor`), *-ize* not *-ise* (`realize, organize`), *-er* not *-re* (`center, theater`), and **`gotten`** as the past participle of *get* (`I've gotten better`), which is American-only and worth one explicit box in the book.

**Recommendation:** do not build "British vs American" units. Build American English as the default throughout, and add a small grey sidebar — *"In British English you may hear…"* — so students recognize British forms in listening tests without producing them.

## 6. What the research says about Thai learners specifically

Boonraksa & Naisena (2022), *A Study on English Collocation Errors of Thai EFL Students*, plus Yumanee's error analysis, converge on a clear picture:

1. **L1 transfer is the number-one source of collocation error.** Thai students build English collocations by translating Thai word-for-word.
2. **Preposition errors dominate.** The highest error counts fall on **noun + preposition** and **adjective + preposition** patterns. Thai students frequently **omit an obligatory English preposition after a verb**, because the Thai verb does not require one. (`*I listen music`, `*Wait me`, `*It depends the weather`.)
3. **Adjective + noun is the worst lexical pattern** — 21.31% of all collocation violations in one study.
4. **Classic literal-translation errors:** `*play internet`, `*play computer` (from เล่นอินเทอร์เน็ต / เล่นคอมพิวเตอร์), and wrong prepositions carried over from Thai's ด้าน / กับ / ที่.

**Consequence for your book:** the Thai column must not be a translation only. Every high-risk item needs a **⚠️ trap line** showing the wrong Thai-influenced form crossed out next to the right one. Seeing `❌ play internet → ✅ go online / use the internet` once is worth more than ten correct examples.

## 7. Delivery design

**Spacing beats volume.** Retention of spaced vocabulary was measured at **three times** that of massed vocabulary, and EFL students spending an average of **three minutes a day** on automatically generated vocabulary activities tripled their long-term retention rate. Whatever your per-lesson load, the recycling schedule matters more.

**Practical loading for a Thai classroom:**
- **8–12 new multi-word items per lesson.** 600 items ÷ 10 = 60 lessons ≈ a two-semester course.
- **Every item must reappear at least 3 more times** in later units (the vocabulary literature generally puts the encounters needed for durable learning in the 8–20 range; the textbook supplies 4 and homework/app review supplies the rest).
- **Recycle across skills, not within a page.** An item introduced in a reading in Unit 3 should return as a speaking prompt in Unit 6 and a writing target in Unit 11.
